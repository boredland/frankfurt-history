# Luitpold-Lichtspiele / Rex

*1912–1977, 64 Kaiserstrasse*

![](../../images/b62f0676c8a65761dcfb68983f4ec35a_original.jpg)

In 1912, this was the entrance to the Luitpold-Lichtspiele, opened on 16 February that year. Part of the Kaiserstraße’s “cinema mile,” this movie theater offered a diverse program of music, variety theatre and film, and was particularly well-known for adventure films by Harry Piel and Richard Talmadge. 

Until 1933, it also exclusively showed silent movies – the last Frankfurt cinema to do so – before finally succumbing to the popularity of the talkies.

*Author: Mai Nguyen und Ogulcan Korkmaz*

Despite frequently changing hands, the Luitpold-Lichtspiele was last operated by the Harley brothers from England. They also ran a small distribution service in the cellar able to supply other neighborhood cinemas, if necessary, at short notice. The Luitpold cinema continued to operate until 1942 when it was forced to close due to war damage.

*Author: Mai Nguyen und Ogulcan Korkmaz*

“The audiences in the Kaiserstraße cinemas were almost exclusively men. (...) Pensioners who hardly knew what to do with their time. Men around forty with briefcases on a break or their way home wanting to quickly keep up on things. ‘Guest workers’ in the evenings and at the weekends. They would hang around the station and in the neighborhood around Kaiserstraße anyway.” - Friedemann Hahn in the Filmkritik magazine
 
In January 1952, the cinema, now called the Rex, reopened with a screening of the Hollywood epic Gone With The Wind(USA, 1939). In 1955, the Rex was equipped with a new wide screen enabling movie goers to enjoy films in the revolutionary CinemaScope format. From 1956 to 1967, one of the co-owners was Siegfried Lubliner, and his innovative ideas influenced the city’s cinema landscape at that time.

Like many cinemas in the Main Station neighborhood, the Rex also changed its program in the 1970s. Now it focused primarily on screening low-budget violence and sex films. 

In the 1960s, in the wake of West Germany’s policy of recruiting workers from abroad, Frankfurt was also home to growing numbers of Italian, Turkish, Greek and Spanish “Gastarbeiter” (literally, “guest workers”). They also wanted to see films from their home countries, a demand fueling an individual cinema culture in West German cities. For instance, according to reports at that time, until the Rex closed in 1977, it showed Turkish films for a Turkish audience. With declining movie audiences in the face of competition from television, this not only offered an important revenue stream but also gave immigrants a chance to ease their homesickness, at least temporally.

*Author: Mai Nguyen und Ogulcan Korkmaz*
