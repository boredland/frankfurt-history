# International Electrotecnical Exhibition

*May 16, 1891–October 19, 1891, from the Central Station to the railings of the former West Stations*

!["Panoramic view of the grounds of the International Electrotechnical Exhibition towards the east from the observation tower. In the background St. Paul's Church and St. Bartholomew's Church"](../../images/a26717b6dfb726a00e62d31fa2259f17_original.jpg)
*Author: unbekannt / unkown | License: Institut für Stadtgeschichte Frankfurt am Main (ISG FFM), ISG FFM, S7Z Nr. 1891-9 | Description: "Panoramic view of the grounds of the International Electrotechnical Exhibition towards the east from the observation tower. In the background St. Paul's Church and St. Bartholomew's Church"*

Opened in Frankfurt on 16 May 1891, the International Electrotechnical Exhibition presented the latest advances in electrical power, as well as telephony and communications. Over 450 exhibitors were showing their new inventions. By the time this trade fair and spectacle for the general public closed on 19 October 1891, it had attracted over one million people. The highlights not only included an artificial waterfall powered by electricity, various lighting displays and steam engines, but also Emil Berliner’s gramophone. Since Thomas Alva Edison was an honorary member of the organizing committee, his Phonograph was also on show. In a specially designed panorama [_> POI Panorama], visitors could marvel at a circular painting of the North German Lloyd steamship SS Lahn entering New York harbor. Thanks to 120 light bulbs, the panorama could also be viewed for an extra charge in the evening.
The exhibition stretched from the Main Station to the then Western Railway Stations. Today, much of this area covering 77,000 square meters, equivalent to nearly 11 soccer pitches, is now occupied by Kaiserstraße from the Main Station to Willy-Brandt-Platz, as well as the Gallusanlage and Taunusanlage.

*Author: Sina Scherer*

As far as the development of cinema is concerned, the exhibition’s principal attraction was on show in the Science and Medicine Hall. From the 18 July 1891, the many exhibits there included Ottomar Anschütz’s Electrotachyscope (also known as an “Electrical Quick-Viewer”). Visitors could insert a 10-pfennig coin (roughly 5 cents today) to watch a sequence of images on a rotating wheel. The scenes offered, for instance, dogs and horses in motion, herons, a mail coach and dancing people. Demonstrations of the Electrotachyscope, made by Siemens & Halske, were held regularly every day between 10am and 9pm. Dr. Franz Stolze, who saw the device at the time, left an account of its effect: “In consequence of being intensely true to nature, the illusion’s impression is so exceptionally appealing and surprising, one can hardly tear oneself away from these realistic and lively images.”
