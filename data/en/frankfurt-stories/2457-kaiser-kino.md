# Kaiser-Kino

*1910–1999, 54 Kaiserstraße*

![Kaiserstraße during a parade in 1912, with the Fantasie Theater on the left and the Hansa-Lichtspiele on the right](../../images/a30dcb4f130cf08e03a560c1a620152e_original.jpg)
*1912 — Author: unbekannt / unkown | License: Institut für Stadtgeschichte Frankfurt am Main (ISG FFM), S7Z Nr. 1912-151 | Description: Kaiserstraße during a parade in 1912, with the Fantasie Theater on the left and the Hansa-Lichtspiele on the right*

The cinema at Kaiserstraße 54 had a checkered history, evident from the long list of its names: Fantasie-Theater, Kaiser-Kino, Astoria, Kaiser-Lichtspiele, Excelsior and Dolly Buster Center.

Originally opened in 1910, it combined a tavern and eatery with a permanent film program. After 1945[aa1] , the new building on the site housed the renamed Kaiser-Lichtspiele with a comfortable cinema auditorium seating 315 people In the 1980s[aa2] , this was then turned into a multiplex screening action and erotic films. Afterwards, successful cinema operator Liselotte Jaeger launched a sophisticated program of international films in original English-language versions to attract audiences to the renamed Excelsior. But according to Jaeger, the location in Frankfurt’s red-light district put off many potential movie goers, especially women. In the 2000s, the business ultimately became an erotic store with a cinema showing sex films.

At the start of the First World War, the Fantasie-Theater was wrecked no less than twice in one day. On the afternoon of 2 August 1914, as reported in the Kleine Presse newspaper, a “Russian” outside the cinema was said to have shouted: “Down with Germany!” and then fled inside. A furious crowd pursued him, damaging the cinema foyer. During the night, the mob returned, stole the French films being shown and again left a trail of devastation behind them. The police had to intervene to restore order. The newspaper commented: “It goes without saying that in these days of intensely heightened national sentiment everything should be avoided which, in this mood, could be taken as insulting and offensive.“

*Author: Hauke Henkel*
