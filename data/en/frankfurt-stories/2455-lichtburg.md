# Lichtburg

*1907–1994, 74 Kaiserstraße*

![Frontal view of the cinema, „Union Theater“, around 1910.](../../images/8603eb3f44fbe9dadd1bf8f74b43d610_original.jpg)
*ca. 1910 — Author: unbekannt / unkown | License: DFF | Description: Frontal view of the cinema, „Union Theater“, around 1910.*

When it opened its doors on 16 November 1907, the Union Theater was Frankfurt’s most elegant venue for the new medium of film. A large arched doorway crowned by an eagle with outstretched wings – the Union symbol – led to a brightly-lit foyer testifying to the splendor of Imperial Germany. The cinema in Kaiserstraße confidently advertised as Germany’s most magnificent movie theater, with a sophisticated program and cutting-edge technology. During longer films, a live narrator explained the context and plot. As early as 1908, there were already sound screenings – though the soundtrack came from a gramophone record. 
 
This was the third picture palace operated by a company that played a crucial role in the history of German cinema: the AKTG, the “General Cinematography Theater Company, Union Theater for living and sound pictures” Later, the AKTG became the PAGU, the Projection Joint Stock Company “Union,” Germany’s first shareholder company in the film business. It operated four cinemas in Frankfurt alone. Over the years, it expanded its operations throughout Germany and even to Brussels, Amsterdam and Strasbourg. In its Kaiserstraße cinema, it pursued a shrewd strategy of “budget luxury.” Performances ran every day from 3pm to 11pm, with the cheapest seats priced at 40 pfennigs – affordable for many and yet offering a more sophisticated atmosphere.
 
In the years after 1912, the cinema changed owners and names several times. In 1914, it became the Cinema Pathé, and by the early 1920s it was called the Kammer-Lichtspiele. But for Frankfurt’s residents, the cinema is best remembered as the Lichtburg, the first movie theatre to reopen in the city after the end of the Second World War.

*Author: Laura Palys*

On the day it reopened, Frankfurt’s Mayor was present in person – an indication of how important the cinema was in society at that time. In the early post-war years, it primarily showed cultural films and documentaries from America as well as the “Welt im Film” newsreels.
 
In the 1960s, the program was dominated by action movies attracting a largely male audience. The Lichtburg was refurbished as a multiplex in the late 1970s To enable more movies to be shown simultaneously, a large auditorium was refurbished as six small “shoebox screens” seating between 40 and 104 people.
 
These years also saw a change in the Lichtburg program. From now on, it was primarily screening pornographic films, such as the Tam Tam film series. In 1971, Ernst Axt, the cinema’s operating manager, faced criminal charges for displaying the promotional film poster of The Ribald Tales of Robin Hood. Ernst Axt, and later the Schwikart family, managed the Lichtburg for the longest period in its history.
 
When the Lichtburg finally closed in 1994, it was Frankfurt’s oldest existing movie theatre. It could look back on nearly 90 years of operation that saw all the developments, highs and lows, of the cinema business.

*Author: Laura Palys*
