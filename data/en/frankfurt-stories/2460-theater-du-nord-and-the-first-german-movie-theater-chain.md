# Theater du Nord and the first German movie theater chain

*1906–unknown, Große Gallusstraße 17*

![Black-and-white photograph of Große Gallussstraße 17 from 1910.](../../images/2eb45242888c13622ff421400f23e85d_original.jpg)
*1910 — Author: unbekannt / unkown | License: Institut für Stadtgeschichte Frankfurt am Main (ISG FFM), S7A Nr. 1998-8423, 0001 | Description: Black-and-white photograph of Große Gallussstraße 17 from 1910.*

On 14 July 1906 at Große Gallusstraße 17, the Theater du Nord opened inside the Hotel du Nord, a Frankfurt institution with a rich tradition. This was the first Frankfurt cinema run by the AKTG – the General Cinematography Theater Company. The AKTG and its successor company, the Projection Joint Stock Company “Union,” were to establish the first cinema chain in Germany.

*Author: Svetlana Svyatskaya*

The Theater du Nord gave no indication of the splendor of the company’s later picture palaces. Instead, the films here could be enjoyed with food and beverages – a place for a glass of beer, a cigar and moving pictures. In the Lichtbild-Bühne magazine in 1932, film producer Lothar Stark recalled the unassuming setting: “A dark entrance to the building, an elongated narrow room seating around 100 people. Wooden benches with no backrests, best seats 50 pfennigs, second-best seats 25 pfennigs, including a one-page program on thin, green wood-pulp paper.”

Just a few months before the cinema opened on 21 March 1906, the AKTG had moved its offices into the rear building behind the hotel. There, Paul Davidson, Hermann Wronker, Julius Wiesbader and Max Bauer founded the first company in Germany pursuing cinema as a modern business proposition – an approach which had a lasting impact on German cinema as an industry.
The company quickly grew. 

By 1912, it had opened four movie houses in Frankfurt alone, including the magnificent Union Theater at Kaiserstraße 74. The Union Theaters (U.T.) soon came to epitomize a sophisticated cinema culture and formed the first German cinema chain – from Frankfurt to Düsseldorf, with ten branches in Berlin, and cinemas in Brussels and Amsterdam. Little is known about the later years of the Theater du Nord. In 1914, it was taken over by a new owner and renamed the Gallus-Lichtspiele cinema.

On 4 September 1909, the AKTG turned into the Projection Joint Stock Company “Union” (PAGU) – Germany’s first shareholder company in the film business. The company offices were now in Kaiserstraße 64, which you have just seen. From there, over the following years, producer Paul Davidson revolutionized the German cinema industry and invented the modern movie star system.

The history of cinema in Frankfurt tells a story of courageous ideas, far-sighted entrepreneurship and passionate film enthusiasts. Many places we have explored no longer exist in the same form today, yet their legacy lives on – in every film we watch today and every cinema we visit.

*Author: Svetlana Svyatskaya*
