# Diorama

*1853–1868, corner of Weserstraße 9 and Niddastraße*

![Historical illustration of the Frankfurt Diorama building with inscriptions and decorative elements.](../../images/efd37a53e97bfa3b6305ded326d2718d_original.jpg)
*Author: unbekannt / unkown | License: “Lebende Bilder einer Stadt”, Hilmar Hoffmann, Walter Schobert, 1995; S. 15, DFF | Description: Historical illustration of the Frankfurt Diorama building with inscriptions and decorative elements.*

Dioramas present seemingly realistic three-dimensional scenes. Although often shown in display cases, such scenes may also occupy entire buildings. The word “diorama,” derived from Ancient Greek, literally means a “see-through image.” The illusion was created by combining semi-transparent painted canvases, three-dimensional figures, decorations and models, while the skillful use of light in front of and behind the transparent paintings intensified the realistic effect.
 
As early as 1832, Paris had a diorama in a dedicated building with a specialized stage and a display able to be changed visually and mechanically. In 1853, a diorama building was also opened in Frankfurt. Constructed at the corner of Weserstraße and Niddastraße by the businessman Johann Conrad Theodor Lattmann, the building comprised two parts set at an obtuse angle to each other. The lighting was very carefully designed. Not only could the light’s intensity be adjusted, but so could its direction from above or behind the canvases painted on both sides. In this way, the mood and even the motif itself seemed to change.

*Author: Mariia Senokosova*

In the 19th century, dioramas were extremely popular – the vivid immersive experience of scenes of distant places and historical events was a rarity at a time without film and photography. The Frankfurt diorama opened with scenes of the Battle of Kulm and the landscape around Lake Lucerne. Later, it also presented vistas of Venice, Heidelberg and Bethlehem. Frankfurt was then a booming commercial and cultural city, and the diorama attracted a broad audience from well-to-do citizens to travelers, students and families interested in art, history and technology. In 1868, with the site required for new housing, the diorama had to close.

Today, most dioramas are found in natural history museums, usually combining taxidermy specimens with replicas of the animals’ natural habitats. Occasionally, dioramas are also still used as stage settings in theatres.
